# How Do NFPA 70E, NFPA 70B and the OSHA General Duty Clause Work Together?

After an electrical incident, the first questions an investigator asks are rarely about the incident itself. They are about the equipment: when it was last maintained, what the records show, and whether the people working on it had any reason to trust its condition. Three documents shape how those questions get answered, and most facilities treat them as three separate compliance projects.

This article explains what NFPA 70E, NFPA 70B and the OSHA General Duty Clause each cover, how the next edition of 70E strengthens the link between maintenance and safe work practices, how an inspector can use all three against a single facility, and how to build one program that satisfies them together.

## What is the relationship between NFPA 70E, NFPA 70B and OSHA?

**NFPA 70B governs how electrical equipment is maintained, NFPA 70E governs how people work on or near it safely, and OSHA's General Duty Clause is the legal hook that lets an inspector hold an employer to both as recognized good practice.** They answer three different questions about the same equipment.

| Document | Question it answers | What it is | Who enforces it |
|---|---|---|---|
| NFPA 70B | Is the equipment maintained so it works as intended? | Consensus standard for electrical equipment maintenance programs | Adopted through contracts, insurers, AHJs and company policy; OSHA does not enforce it directly |
| NFPA 70E | Can people work on or near this equipment safely? | Consensus standard for electrical safety in the workplace | OSHA does not enforce it directly, but treats it as evidence of recognized practice |
| OSHA General Duty Clause (Section 5(a)(1)) | Is the workplace free of recognized hazards likely to cause death or serious harm? | Federal law | OSHA |

Read the table by row and the documents look independent. Read it by column and the overlap is clear: all three are ultimately about whether a facility has controlled the hazard of its own electrical equipment.

## What does NFPA 70B require?

**NFPA 70B requires a facility to run an electrical maintenance program: a documented, ongoing process for inspecting, testing and servicing electrical equipment on defined intervals.** Since the 2023 edition, 70B is a full standard rather than the recommended practice it was for decades, which changed its language from "should" to "shall" and moved it from a reference document to something a facility can be measured against. [[internal link: NFPA 70B electrical maintenance program]]

In practical terms, a 70B-based program rests on a few building blocks:

- An inventory of the electrical equipment in scope
- A condition assessment that ranks equipment by how badly its failure would hurt safety or operations
- Maintenance and test intervals tied to that condition and criticality
- Records of what was done, what was found and what was fixed

That last item is the one that matters most for this article. 70B treats documentation as part of the program, not paperwork on top of it. A test that was performed but not recorded cannot support a maintenance decision later, and it cannot support a safety decision either.

## What does NFPA 70E require?

**NFPA 70E requires employers to establish an electrical safety program and to use risk assessment to decide how, and whether, work on or near exposed energized parts may proceed.** It is built around a hierarchy: eliminate the hazard by putting equipment in an electrically safe work condition first, and treat energized work as the exception that needs a justification, a permit and appropriate protection. [[internal link: NFPA 70E arc flash PPE requirements]]

The standard asks a series of risk questions before anyone opens a cabinet. How much incident energy could be released? What are the shock and arc flash boundaries? What protection does the task require? Every one of those answers depends on an assumption that the equipment will behave as designed. A breaker is assumed to clear a fault in the time the arc flash study calculated. A panel is assumed to contain an arc rather than propagate it.

Those assumptions are where maintenance enters the picture, and 70E has a name for it: the condition of maintenance.

## How does the condition of maintenance connect 70E to 70B?

**70E's condition-of-maintenance provision says that the safety of work on equipment depends on whether that equipment is properly installed and maintained, and the next edition points users toward guidance on how to assess it.** The 2024 edition added an informative annex on assessing the condition of maintenance. Secondary sources describe the next edition as refining that link: an informational note in the condition-of-maintenance provision now directs readers to that annex, and the annex in turn refers to NFPA 70B.

That is a modest change in wording and a meaningful one in practice. It does not turn 70E into a maintenance standard, and it does not make 70B compliance a prerequisite for every task. What it does is write the relationship down inside 70E itself. A facility reading its own safety standard is now pointed at its maintenance standard.

Consider the arc flash calculation. An arc flash study might assume a molded-case breaker trips instantaneously at a given fault level. If that breaker has never been exercised or tested, and its trip settings were never confirmed, the incident energy label on the panel rests on an assumption nobody has checked. The PPE selected from that label may be too light for what the breaker would actually let through. Maintenance, in other words, is not separate from the 70E risk assessment. It is an input to it. [[internal link: arc flash study vs. breaker testing]]

The consequence is that thin maintenance records do not only create a 70B problem. They make it harder to justify the assumptions behind a 70E risk assessment, because the evidence that the equipment is in the condition the study assumes is missing.

## How does OSHA's General Duty Clause fit in?

**The General Duty Clause, Section 5(a)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act, requires every employer to provide a workplace free from recognized hazards that are causing or likely to cause death or serious physical harm.** It is a broad obligation, and it is used where no more specific OSHA standard covers the hazard.

OSHA does not adopt NFPA 70E or NFPA 70B by reference as regulations, and it cannot cite an employer for violating either one as such. OSHA's electrical safety-related work practices rules do exist as their own standards. What the General Duty Clause adds is a route for treating consensus standards as evidence. A recognized national standard can show that a hazard is known in the industry and that feasible ways to control it exist, which are two of the things OSHA has to demonstrate in a General Duty Clause case.

So when an inspector looks at an electrical incident, 70E shows what safe work practice looked like, and 70B shows what maintenance practice looked like. Neither is a regulation. Both are the recognized benchmark for what a reasonable employer should have been doing.

## How would an inspector connect the three?

**An inspector can start from an injury, find a safe-work gap under 70E, trace it to a maintenance gap under 70B, and frame the whole thing as an unabated recognized hazard.** A realistic sequence looks like this:

1. A worker is injured during a task at a switchboard. OSHA opens an investigation.
2. The investigator asks for the energized work permit, the arc flash labels and the risk assessment behind them. Where the facility followed 70E, those exist.
3. The investigator asks what supports the label: when the protective devices were last tested, and whether the one that failed to clear was maintained.
4. The maintenance record is thin. There is no test history for the breaker, or the last one is years old.
5. The gap is now framed as a recognized hazard (an unmaintained protective device) with a known abatement (a 70B-style program) that the employer did not apply.

None of those steps requires OSHA to enforce a consensus standard directly. Each one simply uses the standards as the yardstick. A facility with a clean 70E paper trail and an empty maintenance file has a gap that is easy to explain and hard to defend.

## Where do the three overlap in daily practice?

**The overlap shows up in four places: equipment condition, protective device settings, documentation and who is qualified to do the work.** Looking at each shows where one program can serve all three documents.

### Equipment condition

70B drives the inspection and testing that establishes condition. 70E depends on that condition when it assumes equipment will behave as designed. A single set of infrared, ultrasonic and breaker test results can feed both. [[internal link: infrared thermography electrical inspection]]

### Protective device settings

Coordination and arc flash studies rely on protective device settings. C&H Electric teams often encounter low-voltage breakers left at factory-default settings, which means the study the labels came from does not match the equipment on the wall. Maintenance records that capture as-found settings close that gap.

### Documentation

Test reports, work orders and permits all end up as evidence. A facility that keeps them in one system can answer an investigator, an insurer or a client auditor in minutes instead of days.

### Qualified people

70E expects workers on energized equipment to be qualified, and 70B expects maintenance and testing to be done by competent people. Our technicians at C&H Electric work to NETA standards for testing, which gives a facility a defensible answer to "who did this work and to what procedure."

## What are the common challenges in aligning the three?

**The obstacles are organizational more than technical, because the three documents usually belong to different people.** Safety often owns 70E. Facilities or maintenance owns 70B. Neither owns the OSHA exposure.

### Split ownership

The safety manager writes the energized work permit. The maintenance manager schedules the tests. Nobody compares the two, so an arc flash label can sit on equipment the maintenance team has flagged as overdue.

### Outdated studies

An arc flash study is meant to reflect the system as it stands. Changes to the utility source, protective devices or loads can invalidate it, and maintenance findings are often the first sign something has moved.

### Records that live in too many places

Test reports in a contractor's portal, work orders in a CMMS, permits on paper. When records are scattered, the facility cannot show a coherent history, even if the work was done.

### Budget framing

Maintenance is easy to defer because the cost of skipping it is invisible until something fails. Framing the spend as risk assessment support, not only equipment care, is often what gets it funded.

## Misconceptions about 70E, 70B and OSHA

**Several widely repeated beliefs about these three documents are wrong in ways that cost facilities money or leave them exposed.**

### "OSHA enforces NFPA 70E"

It does not enforce it directly. OSHA enforces its own regulations and the General Duty Clause. 70E matters because it is the recognized benchmark inspectors and courts look to. The practical effect is close to enforcement, but the legal mechanism is different, and the distinction matters when you are preparing a defense or a policy.

### "NFPA 70B is optional guidance"

That was true of the old recommended practice. The 2023 edition is a standard, and its language is mandatory in tone. It is still not law, but insurers, clients and AHJs increasingly treat it as the expectation. [[internal link: NFPA 70B 2023 changes]]

### "If we follow 70E, maintenance is someone else's problem"

70E itself ties safe work to the condition of the equipment. A safety program that ignores whether protective devices work is built on an unchecked assumption.

### "A recent arc flash study covers us"

A study documents the system at a point in time. It does not show that the breakers will operate as modeled today. Maintenance and testing are what keep the study true.

### "The next edition of 70E is already in force"

You will find sources that say it is. Until NFPA issues the new edition and your jurisdiction or program adopts it, the 2024 edition is the baseline. Prepare now, and confirm the status with NFPA before rewriting procedures.

## Recent trends in electrical safety and maintenance

**Standards, insurers and clients are converging on a single idea: safety and maintenance should be managed as one program.** Three trends stand out.

First, 70B's move to a full standard has pushed facilities to formalize maintenance programs, and the cross-reference from 70E's side reinforces that the two documents are meant to be read together.

Second, condition-based approaches are replacing fixed calendar intervals. Thermography, ultrasonic testing and breaker diagnostics feed an assessment of equipment condition, which then sets the interval. That is the same condition language 70E now points to.

Third, more facilities are consolidating records. A single asset register that holds test results, labels, permits and study dates answers the investigator's questions and the insurer's questions with one export.

## How to build one program that satisfies all three

**Start with the asset inventory and let the other pieces hang from it.** Six steps give a facility a practical sequence. [[internal link: electrical maintenance program checklist]]

### Step 1: Inventory and rank your equipment

List switchgear, panelboards, breakers, transformers and motor controls, and rank them by the consequence of failure to people and operations.

### Step 2: Set condition-based intervals

Assign inspection and test intervals by condition and criticality, using 70B as the framework.

### Step 3: Tie arc flash labels to maintenance records

For each labeled piece of equipment, record when the protective device was last tested and what settings were found.

### Step 4: Link permits to equipment condition

Add a condition-of-maintenance check to your energized work permit or job briefing so the person planning the work sees whether the equipment is current on its testing.

### Step 5: Centralize the records

Keep test reports, work orders, permits and studies where one person can pull them together for an auditor, insurer or investigator.

### Step 6: Review on a schedule

Review the whole program annually and whenever the system changes, and confirm the current edition of each standard before you revise procedures.

## Final thoughts

NFPA 70E, NFPA 70B and the OSHA General Duty Clause are not three projects. They are three views of one question: can your facility show that its electrical equipment is maintained and that people work around it safely? The next edition of 70E writes the connection between maintenance and safe work into its own pages, and the General Duty Clause gives inspectors a way to hold employers to both. Facilities that manage them as one program, with maintenance records that support their safety decisions, will find that the documentation they need after an incident is the same documentation that helps prevent one.
