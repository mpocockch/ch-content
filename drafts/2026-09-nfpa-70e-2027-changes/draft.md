# What Is Changing in NFPA 70E's Next Edition?

A crew that has run an energized work permit the same way for a decade is about to find out
that "the way we've always done it" has an expiration date. NFPA 70E's next edition adds
requirements that touch who can be in the room during energized work, what PPE a battery room
actually needs, and how a facility proves a circuit is dead. None of it is optional once it
lands.

This article breaks down what's actually changing in the next edition of NFPA 70E, why the
timing is more complicated than a lot of what's circulating online suggests, and what a
facility can do now to be ready before the requirements become enforceable.

## What Is Changing in NFPA 70E's Next Edition?

**NFPA 70E's next edition adds an additional-person requirement for energized work, new PPE
language for thermal-burn hazards, a new exception for verifying a circuit is de-energized, and
expanded coverage of DC hazards from batteries and solar equipment.** NFPA 70E, the Standard for
Electrical Safety in the Workplace, revises on a three-year cycle (2018, 2021, 2024), and the
next edition in that cycle follows the same pattern of tightening requirements as facilities and
equipment change.

The current, enforceable edition is 2024. The next edition was still moving through NFPA's
standards-development process as this article was written: a second draft, committee action on
public comments, a Technical Meeting where the full NFPA membership can raise motions, and only
then a Standards Council review before an edition is officially issued. That process matters
more than it sounds, because it's also where a lot of the confusion below comes from.

## Why the Timing Matters

**Content claiming the next edition of NFPA 70E has "already taken effect" is circulating, and
facilities that plan around it risk building a compliance program around the wrong deadline.**
NFPA's own published development calendar for this cycle put the membership Technical Meeting in
mid-2026, with Standards Council issuance expected afterward. That timeline does not support an
edition being finalized and enforceable months earlier, whatever date a given article attaches
to it.

That distinction is not academic. A facility that believes a new requirement is already binding
may divert budget and training hours away from what the 2024 edition actually requires today. A
facility that assumes the next edition is years away may get caught flat-footed once it is
issued. The safer posture is the one C&H Electric takes with clients: **treat the 2024 edition as
the current legal and contractual baseline, and treat the next edition's changes as things to
prepare for on a known, structured timeline**, not as a rule to ignore and not as a rule already
in force.

## The New Second-Person Requirement for Energized Work

**The next edition adds a requirement for an additional trained person to be present during
certain energized work performed under an Energized Electrical Work Permit.** This is one of the
better-documented changes heading into the new edition, and it directly changes staffing on jobs
that already require a permit because de-energizing isn't feasible.

The additional person is not a second set of hands on the task. The intent, consistent across
the drafting record, is a dedicated observer:

- Trained in the emergency response procedures relevant to the task, including how to call for
  help and how to respond to a shock or arc-flash event
- Positioned outside the limited approach boundary and the arc-flash boundary, whichever is
  farther, so they are not simultaneously exposed
- Present specifically because energized work concentrates risk in a way that makes a single
  point of failure, one person alone next to a live conductor, harder to justify

**For a crew that has run one-person energized work under a permit for years, this changes
staffing math, not just paperwork.** [[internal link: energized work permit process]] A facility
that waits until the edition is issued to figure out who fills that role, and how they get
trained, will be doing that scramble under a compliance deadline instead of on its own schedule.

## New Thermal and Verification-Testing Provisions

**Two more of the next edition's changes deal with gaps that show up in the field rather than in
the office: PPE that protects against shock but not burns, and voltage testing that doesn't
always prove a circuit is dead.**

### Contact-thermal PPE for burn hazards

Shock protection and burn protection are not the same requirement, and the next edition draws
that line more explicitly. A worker's hands can be protected from electric shock by rubber
insulating gloves and still be exposed to a contact-thermal burn hazard: a hot surface or
component that can burn on contact regardless of whether current is flowing. Battery rooms and
similar equipment are the settings drafting participants have pointed to most often: rubber
insulating gloves satisfy the shock-protection requirement, but they do not, by themselves,
satisfy a separate thermal-burn protection requirement. [[internal link: electrical PPE
categories]] Facilities that treat "insulating gloves" as the complete answer for hand protection
in these spaces should expect that assumption to be tested.

### Absence-of-voltage testing exception

Article 120's requirement to test for absence of voltage before treating a circuit as
de-energized is being expanded with a new exception for situations where a voltage reading alone
doesn't reliably confirm the circuit is dead. Current-transformer secondary circuits and series
airfield lighting circuits are the examples that keep coming up in the drafting discussion. Where
that exception applies, the next edition calls for an additional verification method, such as
testing for absence of current, rather than treating a zero-voltage reading as sufficient on its
own.

## Expanding Coverage to DC Hazards: Batteries, Solar and Capacitors

**The next edition adds dedicated coverage for DC electrical hazards, driven largely by how much
more common battery storage and solar equipment have become in commercial and industrial
facilities.** NFPA 70E has historically been built around AC systems and the shock and arc-flash
hazards that come with them. Battery energy storage systems, photovoltaic (solar) installations
and other DC equipment introduce hazards the standard has not addressed in the same depth:
arcing behavior, energy storage that doesn't de-energize the way a breaker opening does, and duty
cycles that don't map cleanly onto AC assumptions.

**C&H Electric technicians increasingly see facilities add battery storage or rooftop solar
without updating the electrical safety program that governs the rest of the site.** [[internal
link: arc flash risk assessment]] A safety program that was written for switchgear and
panelboards doesn't automatically cover a battery room, and the next edition is a signal that
regulators and standards bodies are catching up to that gap. An audit of DC equipment against
the current program is worth doing well before the edition forces the issue.

## Standards and Compliance

**NFPA 70E works alongside NFPA 70B and OSHA's General Duty Clause, and the next edition
reinforces that connection rather than replacing either one.** [[internal link: NFPA 70B
electrical maintenance program]] NFPA 70B governs the electrical maintenance program itself:
inspection intervals, testing, documentation. NFPA 70E governs the safety practices around
working on or near that same equipment. OSHA does not adopt NFPA 70E by reference wholesale, but
inspectors routinely point to it, along with NFPA 70B, when evaluating whether an employer met
its General Duty Clause obligation to maintain a workplace free of recognized hazards.

The next edition adds language connecting condition-of-maintenance assessment, whether equipment
is in a state that makes energized work newly hazardous, back to that maintenance documentation,
reinforcing that a facility's 70B maintenance records are also evidence for its 70E safety
decisions. A facility with thin maintenance records doesn't just have a 70B problem;
it has a harder time justifying its 70E risk assessments too.

### Where things stand as of publication

The 2024 edition of NFPA 70E is the current, enforceable standard. At the time of writing, the next edition was in the final stages of NFPA's standards-development
process, past the technical committee's second draft and moving through the membership Technical
Meeting and Standards Council review. Facilities should confirm the edition's official issuance status directly with
NFPA before treating any of the specific provisions above as final; drafting-stage requirements
can still change before an edition is issued.

## Common Challenges Preparing Before Issuance

**Getting ahead of a standard that hasn't been issued yet creates a different set of problems
than reacting to one that already has.** The obstacles facilities run into are less about
understanding the changes and more about committing resources to something not yet legally
required.

### Budget and procurement timing

PPE that addresses a contact-thermal hazard, for example, isn't something most facilities
already stock. Ordering it, fitting it, and training on it takes lead time that a facility won't
have if it waits for an effective date to start the process.

### Training capacity

Adding a role, the trained additional person for energized work, means someone has to be
qualified for it, and qualification isn't instantaneous. Facilities with lean electrical staffing
often don't have a bench of people who can absorb a new, safety-critical responsibility without
planning for it.

### Updating documentation ahead of the requirement

Energized Electrical Work Permits, PPE selection tables and lockout/tagout procedures all need to
be reviewed and, in most cases, rewritten to reflect new provisions. Doing that once, deliberately,
is far cheaper than doing it twice: once informally in anticipation, and again for real once
the edition is issued and the details are locked.

## Misconceptions About the Next Edition

**A handful of persistent misunderstandings about this update are common enough to name
directly, because acting on any of them creates real compliance and safety risk.**

### "The 2027 edition of NFPA 70E is already in effect"

This is circulating widely, often with a specific date attached, and it does not match NFPA's own
published development calendar for this cycle. As of this writing, the current enforceable
edition of NFPA 70E is 2024. The next edition was still working through NFPA's Technical Meeting
and Standards Council review process. Treating an unissued edition as binding risks building a
program around requirements that could still change before final issuance. It also risks
neglecting 2024-edition obligations that remain fully in force today.

### "Rubber insulating gloves are enough hand protection in a battery room"

Insulating gloves address shock protection. They do not, on their own, address a contact-thermal
burn hazard from a hot surface or component, which is a distinct hazard the next edition calls
out more explicitly. A hazard assessment that stops at "shock protection" for DC and battery equipment is
incomplete.

### "A zero-voltage reading always proves a circuit is dead"

For most circuits, testing for absence of voltage is the correct and sufficient method. It is not
universal. Current-transformer secondary circuits and series circuits are examples where a
voltage reading alone can be misleading, which is exactly why the next edition adds an exception
requiring an additional verification method in those specific situations.

## Recent Trends

**The changes heading into the next edition track two broader shifts: electrical safety
standards putting more weight on human factors, and standards catching up to equipment that
didn't exist in the same volume when earlier editions were written.** The additional-person
requirement for energized work is part of a longer trend of standards bodies treating isolation
and staffing decisions, not just PPE selection, as core safety controls. And the DC-hazard
expansion reflects how quickly battery storage and solar have moved from niche installations to
routine additions on commercial and industrial sites.

Facilities are also shifting how they track compliance readiness generally, using digital
permit systems and centralized PPE inventories rather than paper forms and supply closets, which
makes absorbing a standards update like this one considerably less disruptive when it lands.

## How to Prepare Now

**Facilities don't need to wait for official issuance to start closing the gap between where
their program is today and where the next edition will require it to be.**

### Step 1: Audit current energized work permits against the additional-person requirement

Identify which tasks are currently performed under a permit with a single qualified worker and no
observer, and start identifying and training candidates for that role now.

### Step 2: Reassess PPE for DC and battery equipment specifically

Don't assume existing shock-protection PPE covers thermal-burn hazards in battery rooms or other
DC equipment areas. Have those spaces assessed on their own terms rather than folded into a
general PPE table built for AC switchgear.

### Step 3: Review absence-of-voltage procedures for edge cases

Identify any circuits in the facility where a voltage-only test could be misleading, such as
current-transformer secondaries or series lighting circuits, and document an additional
verification method for those specific cases now.

### Step 4: Tie the update back into the electrical maintenance program

Use the next edition's reinforced connection between condition-of-maintenance and safety planning
as a reason to confirm NFPA 70B maintenance documentation is current, not just to treat this as a
70E-only exercise.

### Step 5: Confirm official status before finalizing procedures

Check NFPA's own publication status directly before locking in final wording for permits,
training materials or PPE tables, since specific provisions can still shift until an edition is
formally issued.

## Final Thoughts

The next edition of NFPA 70E is not in effect yet, and treating it as though it already is
creates its own kind of risk. But the direction of the changes is clear enough to act on now: a second person for energized
work, sharper PPE requirements for thermal-burn hazards, and a real answer for DC equipment that
didn't exist in the same numbers a decade ago. Facilities that use
this window to update permits, train staff and reassess PPE will meet the requirement when it
lands instead of scrambling to catch up to it.
