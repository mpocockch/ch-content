# What Electrical Testing Does NFPA 99 Require in Hospitals?

In a hospital, the electrical system is part of patient care. When a surveyor asks to see your generator logs, your line isolation monitor tests or your receptacle test records, the question is not whether the equipment works today. It is whether you can prove it has been working, on schedule, for months.

This article breaks down the electrical testing that NFPA 99, the *Health Care Facilities Code*, and the standards it points to expect a hospital to perform and document. It covers how risk categories decide which systems are in scope, the named tests and their intervals, which edition your surveyor actually enforces, where facilities most often come up short, and how to build a testing record that holds up.

## What is NFPA 99?

**NFPA 99 is the code that sets minimum performance requirements for health care facility systems, including the electrical system, based on the risk a failure poses to patients and staff.** It is published by the National Fire Protection Association and covers electrical systems, medical gas and vacuum, HVAC, emergency management and more. Chapter 6 is the electrical chapter.

NFPA 99 does not stand alone. For emergency generators and transfer switches it points to NFPA 110, *Standard for Emergency and Standby Power Systems*. Since the 2021 edition, it points to NFPA 70B, *Standard for Electrical Equipment Maintenance*, for the maintenance program covering the rest of the distribution system. Installation rules live in NEC Article 517. A hospital's testing obligations are the sum of all four, which is why they are easy to lose track of. ## How do risk categories decide what gets tested?

**NFPA 99 assigns every space a risk category based on what happens to a patient if the power serving that space fails, and the category determines whether the space must be served by an essential electrical system.** The facility's governing body makes the assignment through a documented risk assessment. The categories run from most to least severe:

- **Category 1:** failure is likely to cause major injury or death. Operating rooms, ICUs and other critical care spaces.
- **Category 2:** failure is likely to cause minor injury. General patient care spaces.
- **Category 3:** failure is not likely to cause injury but may cause discomfort.
- **Category 4:** failure has no impact on patient care.

Category 1 spaces require a Type 1 essential electrical system (EES). Category 2 spaces require a Type 1 or Type 2 EES. Category 3 and 4 spaces do not require one.

### Type 1 and Type 2 essential electrical systems

A Type 1 EES, the system a typical acute care hospital has, is split into three branches. The **life safety branch** serves egress lighting, exit signs, fire alarm and similar loads. The **critical branch** serves patient care equipment, task lighting and receptacles in critical care areas. The **equipment branch** serves building support loads such as HVAC, medical air and vacuum, and elevators. A Type 2 EES has only the life safety and equipment branches.

That structure matters for testing because each branch is fed through its own automatic transfer switches, and every one of them is on the test schedule.

## What electrical tests does NFPA 99 require?

**The core NFPA 99 testing obligations fall into five groups: the emergency power supply system, transfer switches and breakers on the EES, line isolation monitors, patient care receptacles, and the electrical maintenance program for everything serving Category 1 and 2 spaces.** Each has its own interval and its own record.

### Emergency power supply system

NFPA 99 sends generator testing to NFPA 110. Under the NFPA 110-2010 edition that hospitals are surveyed against:

- **Weekly:** inspect the generator set, including fuel, coolant, batteries and the starting system.
- **Monthly:** exercise the generator under load for at least 30 minutes, at no less than 30% of its nameplate kW rating or at the minimum exhaust gas temperature the manufacturer recommends. The Joint Commission expects monthly tests 20 to 40 days apart.
- **Annually, if monthly tests fall short:** a generator that cannot reach 30% load during its monthly runs needs a supplemental load bank test, at 50% of nameplate for 30 minutes followed by 75% for 60 minutes, 1.5 continuous hours in total.
- **Every 36 months:** a continuous 4-hour run. The Joint Commission requires at least 30% of nameplate load for diesel units.

Light load is the common trap. Many hospital essential loads sit well under 30% of generator capacity, so the monthly test quietly fails its own criteria and wet stacking builds up in the engine. [[internal link: generator load bank testing]]

### Transfer switches and EES circuit breakers

**Every automatic transfer switch on the EES is operated monthly, as part of the generator test, and the breakers in the emergency power path are exercised on their own schedule.** NFPA 110 calls for monthly operation of transfer switches. It also calls for circuit breakers in the emergency power supply system to be exercised annually with the generator off, and for breakers rated over 600V to be exercised every 6 months and tested under simulated overload every 2 years.

The monthly transfer test is also your best evidence that the life safety and critical branches come back within 10 seconds, the restoration time a Type 1 EES is designed to.

### Line isolation monitors

**A line isolation monitor (LIM) must be tested regularly by actuating its test switch, and NFPA 99 sets the interval at no more than one month, or no more than 12 months for a LIM with automated self-test and self-calibration.** LIMs watch isolated power systems, most often in operating rooms and other wet procedure locations where a first ground fault must not interrupt power. The test confirms the monitor alarms when the hazard current crosses its threshold. A LIM is also tested after installation and after any repair or renovation of the isolated system.

C&H Electric teams often find LIM test records that show the button was pressed but not what happened next: whether the alarm sounded, whether the meter read correctly, who checked it. A record with no result is not much of a record.

### Patient care receptacles

**Receptacles in patient care spaces are tested for physical integrity, grounding continuity, correct polarity and blade retention force.** For non-locking receptacles, NFPA 99 sets a minimum retention force of 115 grams (4 ounces) on the grounding blade. Hospital-grade receptacles at patient bed locations are tested after installation, replacement or servicing, and after that at intervals your facility sets from documented performance data.

That last phrase is where the documentation burden sits. A facility that tests on an interval with no failure data behind it has a schedule, not a justification. [[internal link: hospital receptacle testing]]

### The electrical maintenance program

**Since the 2021 edition, NFPA 99 requires every electrical component serving a Category 1 or Category 2 space to be part of an electrical maintenance program that complies with NFPA 70B.** The code's Table 6.9.4.1 lists maintenance intervals by equipment type. Low-voltage power circuit breakers and molded-case breakers are on a 3-year cycle. The annex then points to NFPA 70B for the actual inspection and testing procedures.

In practice this pulls switchgear, panelboards, transformers and breakers serving patient care areas into the same documented program as the generator, rather than leaving them to an informal run-to-failure approach.

**The table below gathers the named intervals in one place, with the standard each comes from.** Use it as a starting point for your own schedule, then check every line against the edition your authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) enforces.

|---|---|---|---|
| Generator set | Inspection | Weekly | NFPA 110 |
| Generator set | Run under load, 30 minutes at 30% nameplate or manufacturer's exhaust temperature | Monthly | NFPA 110 |
| Automatic transfer switches | Operate (transfer the load) | Monthly | NFPA 110 |

## Which edition of NFPA 99 applies to your hospital?

**For a Medicare-participating hospital, the edition that matters is the one CMS enforces, and that is still the 2012 edition of NFPA 99 along with the 2012 Life Safety Code.** CMS adopted both by final rule in May 2016. The Joint Commission surveys to the same editions, which is why its generator requirements cite NFPA 110-2010 rather than a later edition.

NFPA itself has moved on. The 2024 edition is the current published code, and a 2027 edition is working its way through NFPA's process. Your state or local AHJ may also adopt a newer edition than CMS does, in which case you answer to both.

There is one formal bridge between the two worlds. A CMS categorical waiver issued in March 2023 lets a facility use alternate power sources such as health care microgrid systems, provided it meets the 2021 edition of NFPA 99 and the 2023 NEC. A facility has to elect the waiver and document that decision before a survey, not during one.

For a facility manager, the practical answer is to build your program to satisfy the enforced edition first, and to treat the newer editions, including the 2021 maintenance program requirement, as where the bar is heading.

## What do facilities commonly get wrong?

**The most common gaps are not missed tests but tests that were done and cannot be proven.** In C&H Electric's experience, the pattern repeats across hospitals of every size.

- **Records without results.** A log entry that says "LIM tested" or "generator run" without load, duration, readings or a signature does not show compliance.
- **Light-load monthly runs.** The generator runs every month, but at 15% of nameplate. Without exhaust temperature data or an annual load bank test, those runs do not meet NFPA 110.
- **Transfer switches left out.** The generator is tested but not every transfer switch is cycled, especially on branches that are inconvenient to transfer.
- **No documented basis for receptacle intervals.** The facility tests receptacles on a calendar it chose years ago, with no performance data to support it.
- **Distribution equipment outside the program.** The EES gets attention because surveyors ask about it, while the switchgear and panelboards feeding Category 1 spaces go years without testing.

Each of these is a documentation problem as much as a maintenance one. The fix is usually not more testing, but recording the testing you do in a form a surveyor can follow.

## What are the common misconceptions about NFPA 99 testing?

### "NFPA 99 tells you everything you need to test"

It does not. NFPA 99 sets the framework and some intervals directly, but generator and transfer switch testing comes from NFPA 110, and maintenance procedures for the distribution system come from NFPA 70B. A program built from NFPA 99 alone will miss whole categories of required work.

### "The newest edition is the one that applies"

For CMS surveys, the 2012 edition still governs. Following the newest edition is good practice, but citing it to a surveyor will not excuse missing something the 2012 edition requires, and the reverse can also be true where your state has adopted something newer.

### "If the generator runs every month, we're covered"

A monthly run is only compliant if it meets the load or exhaust temperature criteria, runs at least 30 minutes, transfers the load and is documented. Running the generator at a fraction of its rating with no load data is exactly the gap a surveyor reading your logs will look for.

### "The LIM self-tests, so we don't have to"

Automated self-test and self-calibration extends the interval to 12 months. It does not remove the test or the record.

### "Receptacles only get tested when they're installed"

Hospital-grade receptacles at patient bed locations are tested after installation, replacement or servicing, and then again at intervals your facility must justify with documented performance data. "Never again" is not an interval.

## What is changing in NFPA 99 electrical requirements?

**The direction of travel is toward documented, risk-based maintenance across the whole electrical system, not just the emergency power supply.**

- **The 2021 maintenance program requirement.** Section 6.9 brought every component serving Category 1 and 2 spaces into an NFPA 70B program. Even facilities surveyed to 2012 should expect this to become the enforced baseline when CMS next updates its adoption.
- **NFPA 70B became a standard.** Since January 2023, 70B is a mandatory standard rather than a recommended practice, with intervals tied to equipment condition. A hospital program built on 70B can justify longer or shorter intervals from condition data, which suits the "documented performance data" approach NFPA 99 already uses for receptacles. [[internal link: NFPA 70B equipment condition assessment]]
- **The 2027 edition.** Industry coverage of the 2027 cycle describes changes to the electrical chapter, including more explicit requirements to design electrical systems against fire, shock, thermal and natural disaster risks, a more flexible framework for locating isolated power rooms, and a new chapter on cybersecurity for building systems.
- **Microgrids and alternate power.** The 2023 CMS waiver opened the door to non-generator power sources, which bring their own testing and documentation obligations.

## How do you build an NFPA 99 testing program you can document?

**Start from your risk assessment and work outward to a schedule, a procedure and a record for every required test.**

1. **Confirm your risk categories.** Make sure your governing body's risk assessment is current and that you know which spaces are Category 1 and 2.
2. **Map the EES.** List every generator, transfer switch, branch panel and breaker in the essential electrical system, by branch.
3. **Confirm the enforced edition.** Find out which edition CMS, the Joint Commission and your state each enforce, and note where they differ.
4. **Build one schedule.** Put every test from NFPA 99, NFPA 110 and your NFPA 70B program on a single calendar, with an owner for each.
5. **Define the record.** For each test, set the minimum data: date, equipment, load or reading, pass or fail, corrective action and who performed it.
6. **Fix the light-load problem.** If monthly generator runs fall short of 30%, schedule the annual load bank test or start recording exhaust temperature against the manufacturer's figure.
7. **Collect performance data.** Track receptacle and LIM failures so your intervals have a documented basis.
8. **Review the gaps annually.** Walk the records the way a surveyor would, and correct the gaps before someone else finds them.

## Final thoughts

NFPA 99 compliance is rarely lost on a failed test. It is lost on a test that was done but cannot be shown. The generator ran but the load was not recorded. The LIM alarm worked but nobody wrote down that it did. The receptacles were tested once, years ago, on a schedule with nothing behind it.

The facilities that come through surveys cleanly are the ones that treat the record as part of the work. Know which edition you answer to, know which spaces drive your requirements, and make sure every test on your schedule leaves a trail a stranger could follow. It is easier to stay ready than to get ready.
