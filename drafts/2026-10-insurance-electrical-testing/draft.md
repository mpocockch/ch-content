# What Electrical Testing Do Insurance Companies Expect in Commercial Facilities?

When electrical equipment fails in a plant or hospital, the loss rarely stops at the equipment. It becomes a claim, and the first thing an insurer's loss control engineer wants to see is whether the equipment was being tested and maintained at all. Most facilities cannot answer that from a single binder.

This article breaks down what insurers actually ask for, using FM Global's Property Loss Prevention Data Sheet 5-20, *Electrical Testing*, as the reference. It covers what the sheet applies to, which tests it recommends and how often, what the acceptance criteria look like, how the October 2025 update changes infrared scanning, and how to turn all of it into a program you can document.

## What electrical testing do insurance companies expect?

**Insurers expect a documented program of visual inspections, infrared surveys and periodic electrical tests on the equipment whose failure would stop operations or cause a fire.** FM Global, one of the best-known commercial property insurers, states the logic plainly in Data Sheet 5-20: the majority of electrical losses trace back to poor operating conditions, such as overload and loose connections, or to the environment, such as poor ventilation, corrosion, contamination and stored combustibles. Most of those losses, the sheet says, can be prevented by regular visual inspection, good housekeeping and basic electrical tests like infrared scanning and insulation-resistance measurement.

Two caveats matter before going further. First, Data Sheet 5-20 is a set of loss prevention recommendations. How a recommendation becomes a condition of your coverage depends on your carrier, your policy and your loss control engineer, so treat this article as a map of what a major insurer considers good practice, not a reading of your policy. Second, other carriers publish their own guidance. Ask yours for it in writing. [[internal link: electrical maintenance program checklist]]

## What does FM Global Data Sheet 5-20 cover?

**Data Sheet 5-20 covers inspection, testing, maintenance and testing-interval guidance for low-voltage electrical equipment, including supply and distribution transformers of 5 MVA and below and their associated switchgear.** It does not apply to low-power devices such as data and communication systems or process controls, and it points to separate FM data sheets for larger transformers, emergency generators, motors and drives, and switchgear and circuit breakers.

It addresses breakers, contactors, fused switches, transfer switches, bus ducts and cables, transformers, generators, UPS systems, motors and grounding. Its definition of low voltage follows IEEE 141, which means 600V or less. The sheet was completely revised in October 2016 and has had interim revisions since, the latest in October 2025.

## What visual inspections and housekeeping do insurers look for?

**Insurers expect qualified personnel to walk electrical rooms roughly monthly or quarterly and confirm that equipment is clean, cool, dry and tight.** Data Sheet 5-20 sets the frequency by environment: equipment exposed to dirty or humid atmospheres should be inspected more often. The inspection uses only the senses (sight, sound, smell and touch) with equipment in normal operation, and the sheet says it should take no more than 10 minutes to walk through the average main electrical room.

The four conditions are specific:

- **Clean:** no dust on or in switchgear cubicles, no trash on top of cubicles, and no combustible material stored within 5 ft (1.5 m) of switchgear.
- **Cool:** no discolored or warped breaker casings, no blistered paint, and no temporary fans or propped-open doors being used to cool equipment. Switchroom ambient temperature should stay at or below 86°F (30°C), or between 68°F and 77°F (20°C to 25°C) where solid-state relays, process controllers or VRLA batteries are present.
- **Dry:** no rust stains, no watermarks on walls or floors that suggest past leaks or flooding, no tarpaulin draped over gear, no condensation, and no water or sewage pipes running through the room.
- **Tight:** cubicle doors closed, breaker casings intact, no signs of vermin, cable penetrations fire stopped, and connections torqued to the manufacturer's values.

The temperature guidance has a hard number behind it. The sheet notes that for every 18°F (10°C) rise above rated operating temperature, a halving of insulation life can be expected.

## How often should electrical equipment be tested?

**Data Sheet 5-20 sets most electrical tests at every 3 to 5 years, with infrared surveys annually and fluid-filled transformer oil testing every 1 to 2 years.** The sheet is explicit that these frequencies are a general guide. It tells the facility to adjust based on equipment type, duty, failure history, criticality and condition. Where the manufacturer or an industry standard gives different intervals, those apply first.

| Equipment | Activity | Recommended frequency |
|---|---|---|
| All electrical equipment | Infrared survey | Annually |
| Switchgear, panels, motors | Visual inspection | Monthly or quarterly |
| Grounding conductors | Visual inspection | Every 1 to 3 years |
| Critical and main low-voltage breakers | Exercise, inspect, service, test | Every 3 to 5 years |
| Motor contactors (over 50 hp, or critical process) | Inspect, service, test | Every 3 to 5 years |
| Switches, fuses and fused switches | Exercise, service, test | Every 3 to 5 years |
| Automatic transfer switches | Functional test by simulating loss of normal power | Every 3 to 5 years |
| Critical bus ducts and cables | Electrical tests | Every 3 to 5 years |
| Fluid-filled transformers | Fluid screen tests and dissolved gas analysis | At least every 1 to 2 years |
| Fluid-filled transformers | Electrical tests | Every 3 to 5 years |
| Dry-type transformers | Inspect, clean, service, test | Every 3 to 5 years |
| Emergency generators | Run under load | Monthly |
| Emergency generators, UPS | Functional and electrical tests | Every 3 to 5 years |
| Induction motors | Visual inspection | Monthly |

## What do the electrical tests actually measure?

**The tests measure three things: the resistance of bolted connections, the resistance of insulation, and, for protective devices and transformers, whether they operate as designed.** The sheet gives acceptance criteria for each equipment type, to be used as a reference when no manufacturer guidance or industry standard is available.

### Low-voltage circuit breakers

For breakers, the recommended tests are low resistance on bolted connections with a low-resistance ohmmeter, contact resistance on each pole, insulation resistance phase-to-phase and phase-to-ground with the breaker open and closed, and primary current injection to verify the protection functions operate within their tolerance band. Trip and close coil voltage checks are listed as optional. Resistance readings between similar bolted connections should not differ by more than 50%, contact resistance should not deviate by more than 50% of the lowest value between poles, and insulation resistance should be at least 100 megohms. The sheet adds that breakers with direct tripping can only be tested by primary current injection. [[internal link: what is breaker testing]]

### Fuses, switches and transfer switches

Fused switches get the same resistance and insulation tests, plus a fuse resistance measurement, where fuses must not deviate by more than 15% between phases. The sheet also says that when blown fuses are replaced, all three phases should be replaced at the same time with like fuses. Automatic transfer switches get a functional test, an infrared check in both positions, and a check of the mechanical interlocks. The sheet warns that improper lubrication is a common cause of switch binding, so use only the manufacturer's lubricant.

### Transformers

For fluid-filled units, the sheet calls for fluid screen tests and dissolved gas analysis at least every 1 to 2 years regardless of fluid type, with the results trended. Insulation resistance should be at least 100 megohms on the low-voltage winding and 5,000 megohms on the high-voltage winding, and winding power factor should be below 3% for most fluid-filled units. Transformers contaminated with or suspected of containing PCBs get a PCB check every 5 years, with content not to exceed 50 ppm. Dry-type transformers add a polarization index, which should not be less than 1.0, and the sheet wants cooling fans, temperature switches and alarms confirmed working. [[internal link: transformer testing and dissolved gas analysis]]

### Generators, UPS and motors

Emergency generators should be started monthly and, for diesels, run for 30 minutes under load at a minimum of 30% of nameplate rating or the manufacturer's recommended minimum, whichever is more severe. That load requirement exists to prevent wet stacking, where unburnt fuel residue accumulates on the exhaust valve stem and hardens into a resin that can cause stuck valves and bent push rods. Winding insulation resistance corrected to 40°C should be at least 5 megohms, and the dielectric absorption ratio should not be less than 1.4. Induction motors get a monthly visual check and the same electrical tests every 3 to 5 years where no viable equipment contingency plan exists.

### Grounding

Ground-earth resistance should be tested when connections are first installed and again when conditions warrant: protective relays not operating correctly for ground faults, surge equipment failing to protect, step and touch potentials present, signal noise on electronic equipment, or physical deterioration of a grounding conductor. The sheet's target is a resistance that does not exceed 1 ohm, or the recommended value under applicable industry guidelines.

## How does infrared thermography fit in?

**Data Sheet 5-20 recommends an infrared survey of all electrical equipment annually, and the October 2025 revision added a table for judging how serious a finding is.** Infrared scanning is the sheet's preferred method for catching improperly made connections, because installers who skip a torque wrench tend to under-torque, and a loose connection heats up before it fails.

The new table is a fallback for when no manufacturer guidance or industry standard applies. It compares the temperature difference between similar components under similar loading, or between a component and ambient air:

| Difference between similar components | Difference from ambient | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|
| Under 3°C (37°F) | Under 10°C (50°F) | Continue preventive scanning |
| 4°C to 15°C (39°F to 59°F) | 11°C to 20°C (52°F to 68°F) | Probable deficiency. Repair as time permits, for example shut down within months |
| 4°C to 15°C (39°F to 59°F) | 21°C to 40°C (70°F to 104°F) | Monitor until corrective measures can be accomplished, for example shut down within weeks |
| Over 15°C (59°F) | Over 40°C (104°F) | Major discrepancy. Repair immediately, for example shut down within hours |

Record the findings, the loading at the time of the scan and the repair dates. The sheet also covers infrared view ports in arc-resistant switchgear, noting that the arc-resistant integrity of the gear must not be compromised and that ports should be placed where problems are likely, such as the rear panels where main bus bar bolted connections and cable terminations can be viewed.

## What are the common misconceptions about insurer testing requirements?

**The misconceptions come from treating one data sheet as a universal rulebook, or from assuming an annual scan is the whole requirement.** Four come up repeatedly.

### "Insurers only want an annual infrared scan"

The annual survey is one line in a longer program. Breakers, switches, transfer switches, transformers, generators and grounding each carry their own tests, and the visual inspections run monthly or quarterly.

### "The intervals are fixed"

The sheet describes its own frequencies as a general guide and tells facilities to adjust for duty, failure history, criticality and condition. A breaker with a trip history in a dirty environment warrants more attention than the same breaker in a clean room.

### "Meeting the data sheet means meeting NFPA 70B"

They overlap in purpose but are different documents. The reference list in Data Sheet 5-20 names NFPA 70 (the NEC), several IEEE standards and NETA's Maintenance Testing Specifications, and it does not list NFPA 70B. A program built only to satisfy an insurer may still leave you short of what 70B expects, and the reverse can be true. [[internal link: NFPA 70B maintenance requirements]]

### "Testing means shutting the plant down"

Visual inspection, infrared scanning and ultrasonic scanning are done with equipment energized. The resistance and insulation tests generally require de-energizing the equipment under test, which is why the sheet prioritizes critical and main devices rather than every breaker in the building.

## What recent changes affect insurer testing expectations?

**The most recent change is the October 2025 infrared table, and the sheet also leans on ultrasonic scanning, trending and condition-based intervals.** Beyond the new thermography guidance, three themes run through the document:

- **Ultrasonic scanning:** A trained technician can scan energized equipment with a handheld device, listening around door seals and vents or through the enclosure, to detect possible arcing in breakers, switches and contacts. It works best with sound imaging software.
- **Trending over snapshots:** For dissolved gas analysis, the sheet says changes in gas generation rates are more sensitive indicators of incipient problems than absolute concentrations, and that sampling technique can invalidate results.
- **Studies behind the testing:** The sheet expects load flow, fault current and protection coordination studies to be complete, and repeated when a utility feeder or transformer is added, transformers are paralleled, or motors or in-house generation above 50 hp (37 kW) are added.

## How do you build a program insurers will accept?

**Build it from an equipment inventory, rank by criticality, apply the intervals above as a starting point, and keep records of everything.** A practical sequence:

1. **Inventory the equipment** in scope: breakers, transfer switches, transformers, generators, UPS systems, motors and grounding.
2. **Rank by criticality.** The sheet puts maintenance priority on critical and main devices. Decide which failures would stop production or patient care.
3. **Request your insurer's guidance in writing.** Confirm whether they follow Data Sheet 5-20 or their own schedule.
4. **Set intervals** from the manufacturer's instructions first, then industry standards, then the data sheet table.
5. **Run the visual inspections** on a documented schedule and walk the rooms against the clean, cool, dry and tight list.
6. **Schedule the infrared survey and the periodic tests** with qualified, certified technicians. Our technicians at C&H Electric are certified to NETA QEMC standards and work to the same acceptance criteria described above.
7. **Record the results and the repairs.** Keep readings, loading conditions, findings and completion dates together so the trend is visible.
8. **Review annually.** Adjust frequency for failure history and condition, and repeat studies after system changes.

## Final thoughts

Insurers want to know that the equipment that can burn down or shut down your facility is being looked at on a schedule, tested against criteria, and documented. FM Global's Data Sheet 5-20 gives a clear picture of what that looks like: monthly or quarterly walk-throughs, annual infrared surveys, and electrical tests every 3 to 5 years on the critical gear, with the schedule tightened or relaxed according to condition and criticality.

Compare your current records to that picture and close the gaps while the equipment is still running. That conversation goes better with your insurer before a loss than after one. [[internal link: electrical preventive maintenance]]
