Your electrical panel could look completely dry and still be dangerous to turn back on.

Floodwater rarely arrives clean. Silt, sewage, road salt, and chemical residue reach the
contacts and insulation inside a panel long after the room looks dry, and corrosion keeps
working after the visible water is gone.

A breaker can close and look normal in a quick check while its trip mechanism no longer
responds correctly under an actual fault. The only way to know is to test it, not eyeball it.

Here's what a real flood response involves:
→ De-energize first, without anyone entering standing water or touching a wet panel
→ Call the utility if water reached the meter or service entrance, before anyone else touches it
→ Insulation resistance and dielectric testing by equipment type, not a visual check
→ Replace what NEMA's own guidance says can't be reliably reconditioned, like submerged breakers with electronic trip units
→ Re-energize incrementally, with a thermal scan once equipment is back under load

NEMA's published guidance on water-damaged equipment is direct: electronic-trip breakers and
similar devices that were submerged generally get replaced, not cleaned and put back in
service.
